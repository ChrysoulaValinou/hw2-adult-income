Title: The Marriage Premium and Demographic Disparities in Wage Distribution: An Empirical Analysis.
Abstract: This paper investigates the underlying demographic factors contributing to wage disparities, focusing on the well-documented "marriage premium" and the persistent gender wage gap. Using census data from the mid-1990s, we analyze how marital status, race, and gender interact to influence the probability of high-income categorization (defined as earning >$50K annually).

Introduction: A consistent finding in labor economics is that married individuals, specifically those classified as "Married-civ-spouse," generally report significantly higher household and individual incomes compared to their "Never-married" or "Divorced" counterparts. For men, this marriage premium is often attributed to the specialization of labor within the household, allowing married men to dedicate more time and effort to human capital accumulation and career advancement. 

Conversely, the data reflects historical disparities regarding gender and race. In the 1994 economic landscape, female workers and minority groups faced structural barriers that lowered their statistical likelihood of reaching the top income brackets. Women, regardless of education, often experienced a "motherhood penalty" rather than a marriage premium, highlighting how demographic features in the dataset carry deep historical and social contexts that heavily skew predictive models.
