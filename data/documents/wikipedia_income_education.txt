Socioeconomic status (SES) and income distribution in the United States are heavily influenced by educational attainment. According to extensive demographic research and census data, education is one of the most reliable predictors of financial success. Individuals who complete higher education, particularly bachelor's, master's, or professional degrees, consistently exhibit higher lifetime earnings and are significantly more likely to cross the $50,000 annual income threshold.

The mechanism behind this correlation is twofold. First, advanced degrees act as a gateway to specialized occupational sectors, such as executive management (Exec-managerial) and professional specialty roles (Prof-specialty). These fields not only offer higher base salaries but also come with benefits, stock options, and structured career progression. Second, higher education provides a buffer against economic downturns, resulting in lower unemployment rates and fewer gaps in employment history.

In the context of the 1990s United States economy, the wage premium for college graduates was actively expanding. The transition from a manufacturing-based economy to an information and services economy meant that manual labor and roles requiring only a high school diploma faced wage stagnation. Consequently, the divide between individuals with a high school education and those with university degrees became a defining feature of the American class structure.
